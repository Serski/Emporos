# Emporos

A pixel-art trading game for phones. Ariston, a young trader from Rhodes, sails out in 290 BC with a small ship, a sea loan and a crew that has to eat. *Emporos* is the Greek word for a merchant who travels by sea. It is a working title.

There are nine chapters, each a voyage further out from Rhodes, and **all nine are playable**: Halicarnassus in the spring, Miletus in the summer, Athens in the autumn; in a bigger ship the next year, Corcyra in the spring, Taras in Italy in the summer, and Syracuse in Sicily in the autumn; and, the year after, in a grain ship with a second deck, Cyprus in the spring, Tyre in the summer and, last of all, Alexandria in the autumn.

## Play

Open `index.html` in a browser. It is a single file with nothing to install, and progress saves in the browser.

To put it online for free with GitHub Pages (public repositories only on the free plan): in the repository, go to **Settings → Pages**, choose **Deploy from a branch**, then pick `main` and `/ (root)`. The game appears at `https://serski.github.io/Emporos/` a minute or two later.

## Chapter I: Halicarnassus

Antimachos the banker has lent Ariston 150 drachmas on a sea loan. Bring the ship home and he owes 195. If she sinks, he owes nothing. A round trip takes six days at sea and about fifteen minutes to play.

- **The quay.** Tap a merchant, on the quay or in the picture, to buy, sell and haggle. Prices drop as you sell more of one thing, and haggling too hard makes them worse. A marker over a stall means that merchant buys something you are carrying.
- **The hold.** The ship is a grid, seen from above, with the quay beside it. Everything you take aboard needs squares: cargo, barley, water jars, the bolt-thrower, and the gear of anyone you hire. Drag to move, tap to turn, or turn mid-drag with a second finger. The bolt-thrower fits only in the bow. Fragile cargo breaks in rough water unless it touches something soft.
- **The crew.** Everyone aboard eats and drinks every day. A sack of barley or a jar of water does ten people for a day. Rowing doubles what the rowers drink. Thirsty sailors row badly and fall sick.
- **Hiring.** A Rhodian slinger in Rhodes. A Cretan archer and a doctor from Cos in Halicarnassus. Fighters shoot before pirates can board. With the doctor aboard, nobody dies of their wounds.
- **At sea.** The clock runs from dawn to dusk. Watch the wind, row when you are becalmed, cut the rations if you must, and reach the next beach before dark. Ports and night camps stop the clock.
- **Trouble.** A squall on the way out, and a pirate hemiolia on the way home. Run, pay them off with cargo, or fight.
- **Coming home.** Sell, repay the banker, and see how the season went.

## Chapter II: Miletus

Summer, 290 BC. Miletus is north, and in summer the wind blows from the north every day. Going there you row against it; coming home it is behind you. The round trip takes about eight days at sea, with stops at Kos on the way out and the way back, and about twenty minutes to play.

- **The north wind.** Headwinds most of the way out. Row the calm hours of the early morning, and reach the next beach before dark. A night anchored at sea leaves the crew exhausted, and they row badly the next day.
- **The heat.** In summer the crew drinks a third more, unless the ship has an awning.
- **Fittings.** The arsenal at Rhodes sells an oar bench, which makes rowing a quarter faster and must lie against the side of the hull; a water pithos, which holds three jars of water in two squares; and a deck awning. Fittings stay with the ship.
- **New ports.** Kos, with its wine, its cloth and the sanctuary of Asklepios. Miletus, with its wool, purple dye, carved couches, and a philosopher who will talk to anyone.
- **New hands.** A pilot from Kos, who makes the ship faster, and a Thracian swordsman in Miletus, who is worth three men and frightens pirates. Lykos the doctor is back on Kos, where he is from.
- **On the way.** Jars from a wreck, if you have room for them. A king’s warship that wants a toll: pay it, argue that Rhodes pays no king, or send the officer a jar of wine. The oracle at Didyma will answer one question about the voyage, and it is worth listening to.
- **Money.** Antimachos offers another sea loan, which you can take or refuse. His wife wants a Milesian couch, and he will pay well for one.

## Chapter III: Athens

Autumn, 290 BC: one more voyage before the winter closes the sea. West across the islands to Delos, then on to Piraeus, the harbour of Athens, and home the same way. About nine days at sea, and twenty-five minutes to play.

- **Short days.** In autumn the day runs out sooner, and a night at sea is cold. Men fall sick from it, and the ship goes slower.
- **Freight.** Philonides, a shipping agent in Rhodes, pays you to carry sealed crates: one for Delos and one for Piraeus. In Piraeus another agent has one for Rhodes. Each takes two squares, and you are paid on delivery. If pirates take a crate, you pay what it was worth.
- **The gale.** The open crossing from Astypalaia to Delos is the season of gales, and one always comes. Throw the heaviest cargo over the side, hold on and bail, or run for the lee of Amorgos and lose most of the day. A ship's carpenter, for hire in Rhodes, keeps her together. By the law of Rhodes, cargo thrown over to save a ship is a loss shared by everyone with goods aboard, so with freight in the hold the owners pay you back a share.
- **New ports.** Delos, the holy island of Apollo, which grows nothing and buys everything. Piraeus, with oil, painted cups, books, Hymettus honey and Pentelic marble, city grain wardens who pay well for wheat, a tax of a fiftieth on cargo, and a thin man under a colonnade with opinions about wealth.
- **Packing.** Nothing soft is sold in Athens, so bring sponges from Rhodes to pack the painted cups for the rough water home.
- **Pirates.** A hemiolia may be waiting off Amorgos on the way home. The talk on the quay at Delos tells you whether it is. A Cretan archer is for hire there.
- **A bigger ship.** Nausikrates the shipwright will build a proper merchant ship over the winter, longer than Elpis and with a third more hold, for 600 drachmas and Elpis in part exchange. Order her at the end of the season. If you are short, Antimachos lends the rest against the new ship, if you can put 200 down.

## Chapter IV: Corcyra

Spring, 289 BC, in a bigger ship. West out of the Aegean to Corinth, across the Isthmus, and up the coast to Corcyra, at the edge of the Greek world. About nine days at sea, and thirty minutes to play.

- **Tyche.** The ship ordered at the end of Chapter III is ready. She is Elpis's width and two rows longer: 40 squares of hold, against Elpis's 30, with a longer quay beside her, so on a phone her squares are as big as Elpis's. She has a deck, so hired men keep their gear up there instead of in the hold. She sails faster than Elpis, rows at half the strength, is harder for pirates to board, and comes with two more sailors. If you could not order her in the autumn, Nausikrates will still sell her at the start of the season, or you can sail Elpis. A captain with a second ship needs no help text in the hold, so it is gone.
- **New hands.** From this chapter on, men sign on over the winter to fill the places of anyone who did not come home. The dead stay dead.
- **Corinth.** Haulers drag the ship across the Isthmus on the diolkos, the paved road between the two seas, for a fee each way. Corinth buys grain, wine and incense, sells its famous bronze, and has a pilot for hire who knows the Gulf. An old man in the sun remembers a philosopher who lived in a jar.
- **Corcyra.** Amber from the far north, Molossian hounds from Epirus (they eat as much as a man, and you will not throw one overboard), Epirote cheese, and fir spars three squares long. The queen's steward pays well for incense and luxuries.
- **Men in the water.** Four Corinthian sailors clinging to a wreck. Take them aboard, and feed them, or sail on. Corinth may hear about it either way.
- **The way home.** At Ithaca, go back through the Gulf and pay the haulers again, or round Cape Malea, a day longer, where the wind is famous. The old woman in the cave on Ithaca will tell you, for an offering, whether the cape is awake this year.
- **Illyrians.** Raiders in light boats may be waiting among the islands off Leukas. The talk on the quay at Corcyra tells you whether they are. An Epirote spearman is for hire there, and Lykos the doctor has spent the winter in Rhodes.

## Chapter V: Taras

Summer, 289 BC. Round Cape Malea and up the outside of Greece to Zakynthos, then two days across the open sea, out of sight of land, to Croton and Taras in Italy. About twelve days at sea, and thirty-five minutes to play.

- **Only Tyche can go.** Elpis is a coast boat, and nobody will take her out of sight of land. If you still have her, Nausikrates has the new ship on his slipway, and this time Antimachos lends the whole price.
- **The open sea.** From Zakynthos you sail until dark, sleep at sea under sail, standing watches, and sail on. There is no beach and no spring, and nothing can go on the beach because there is none: the hold is all you have. What you did not cover by dusk is added to the next day. A night under sail leaves the crew tired, unless they rested through a calm.
- **Becalmed.** The wind may die in the middle of the crossing. Row on through the heat, which costs water and, without an awning, may knock a man down; rig the sail for shade and wait, and reach Italy later; or pour a jar of wine to Poseidon, which works about half the time.
- **Water.** In the summer heat everybody drinks more, rowers drink twice as much, and animals die of thirst first. The sail check counts the days with no spring, not the whole voyage.
- **Croton.** Once the strongest city in Italy, with its wrestlers on the beach, a temple of Hera alone on its cape, pitch and fir spars from the Bruttian forests, and an old Pythagorean who will not eat beans. Milon the wrestler is for hire, and fights like two men.
- **Taras.** The richest Greek city in the west, on a neck of land between the sea and a lagoon. It buys incense, perfume, papyrus, bronze and amber, and sells Tarentine wool, purple, painted Apulian vases and a cavalry horse, which takes four squares and eats and drinks like two men. Antimachos wants it for his son. Archippos, a pilot who has crossed forty times, is for hire. An old man remembers a philosopher who made a wooden bird that flew.
- **The way home.** Back across the open sea, where men from a dead king's army may be waiting off the cape of Hera. At Zakynthos, go home through the Gulf and pay the haulers at Corinth, or round Cape Malea again.
- **Free pitch.** The springs on Zakynthos run with pitch, and the shipwrights in Rhodes buy it.

## Chapter VI: Syracuse

Autumn, 289 BC: the last voyage of the year, and the last in Tyche. South-west to Karpathos and along the north coast of Crete to Kydonia, then three days across the open sea to Syracuse, and home the same way before the sea closes for the winter. About eleven days at sea, and forty minutes to play.

- **The open sea in autumn.** Two nights under sail each way, and the days are short. Autumn nights at sea are cold, and men fall sick from them, so a doctor earns his fee.
- **A gale with nowhere to hide.** In the middle of the crossing a gale may come down. Throw cargo, hold on and bail (a carpenter keeps her together), or run before it under a scrap of sail, which breaks little but blows you back most of a day, and may cost another night at sea.
- **Kydonia.** A city of archers, quinces and arguments under the White Mountains. Dittany, the Cretan herb that doctors everywhere pay for, and quinces. Kydas the archer is home, and Erginos, a pilot who knows the way to Sicily, is for hire. An old man from Knossos says that all Cretans are liars.
- **Syracuse.** The old king has just died, and the people are pulling down his statues and selling the bronze by weight. The statue works in Rhodes pay well for a dead king's bronze. Hyblaean honey, salt tunny from the strait, a rich house that buys luxuries, a doctor who buys dittany, and an old soldier who fought for the king in Africa.
- **The Campanians.** Eight of the dead king's soldiers want passage to Crete. They pay well when they land, take no squares, and eat and drink like your crew. On the way, they may decide they would rather have the ship. Stand up to them, with whatever crew you have, or give them back their fare.
- **Cretan pirates.** On the coast of Crete, pirates may come out from behind a cape. With a Cretan aboard you can let him talk to them first.
- **The way home.** At Amnisos, below Knossos, go home by way of Karpathos, or run straight for Rhodes across the open sea before a strong wind.

## Chapter VII: Cyprus

Spring, 288 BC, in a grain ship. East along the coast of Lycia, round the cape of swallows, across the open sea to Paphos, the city of Aphrodite, and on to Kition, where the Phoenicians are. About ten days at sea, and forty minutes to play.

- **The grain ship.** Nausikrates has finished the ship he was drawing in the sand: Tyche's hull, deeper, with a whole second deck for cargo on top of the hold. She has 40 squares below and 34 on deck, sails faster than anything in the harbour, hardly rows at all, and pirates need ladders to board her. She costs 1,200 drachmas and Tyche in part exchange; Antimachos lends the rest if you can put 400 down. You can also sail Tyche again.
- **The deck.** The hold has a second page. Switch between the hold and the deck above the grid, drag things onto either one, or select a thing and send it below or up on deck. The crew packs the vital and the valuable below and the cheap and the bulky on deck. Cargo on deck takes the weather: in rough water or a gale the sea may wash some of it over the side. Animals are tied down, and nobody gambles with another man's crate.
- **King Ptolemy's toll.** Cyprus belongs to the king of Egypt, and his warships patrol the coast of Lycia. Pay the toll, send the officer a jar of wine, or remind him that Rhodes called his king the Saviour after the siege.
- **The cape of swallows.** A gale may come off the Lycian mountains as you round the cape. Holding on with a full deck costs deck cargo; running for the lee of the Swallow Islands costs time.
- **Paphos.** The sanctuary of Aphrodite, whose image is a cone of black stone, and a priestess who buys incense, purple and amber. Copper from the mines, which the statue works need for the god's head, and kyprinon, the perfume of Cyprus. An old sculptor tells the story of Pygmalion.
- **Kition.** A Phoenician city with a pink salt lake full of flamingos and ships with horses' heads on their bows. Sidonian glass, Lebanon cedar for the shipwright, salt, a merchant who buys everything, Mago the Phoenician pilot, and an old captain who has sailed past the Pillars of Herakles.
- **The way home.** At Myra, go by way of Patara, or run straight for Rhodes before a strong wind, which is quicker and hard on whatever is on deck.

## Chapter VIII: Tyre

Summer, 288 BC, in the grain ship. King Ptolemy of Egypt has sent his fleet up the coast of Phoenicia to take the cities Demetrius still holds. Sidon has opened its gates to him; Tyre, on its island, has shut them. East to Megiste, the last Rhodian island, then three days across the open sea to Sidon, one day south to Tyre, and home against the summer wind. About eleven days at sea, and forty minutes to play.

- **What a soldier could use.** The garrison in Tyre pays several times the price for grain, pitch, fir spars, cedar, copper and bronze. King Ptolemy's patrol, off the coast between Sidon and Tyre, takes all of it. The sail check and each thing's description say what aboard counts.
- **The patrol, going in.** Let them search the ship and lose the war goods, send the officer a present and hope he does not look (he usually does not), or crowd on sail and run for Tyre. A big, light ship in a fair wind gets away more often. If they catch you, they take the war goods and a fine, and Rhodes hears of it. If you get through, the garrison cheers you in.
- **The patrol, coming out.** Ships leaving Tyre pay the king a tenth of their cargo: pay it, bribe the officer instead, or run for it and pay double if they catch you.
- **The open sea in summer.** Two nights under sail each way, in the heat. Megiste has a cistern; there is no water after it. A calm may come in the middle of the crossing.
- **Sidon.** The king of Egypt's city now, with his eagle over the gate and an officer on the quay writing down the names of ships. Glass furnaces under the mountains of Lebanon, cedar coming down from them, and a merchant who buys what Cyprus and Greece send east.
- **Tyre.** An island city with walls straight up out of the sea, joined to the shore by the road Alexander built to take it. Tyrian purple, dyed twice, and balsam from the gardens by the Jordan, which nobody can get out of the city any more. The quartermaster of the garrison, a merchant tired of fish, a priest of Melqart at the temple with two pillars, and Philippos, a Macedonian soldier who has decided his war is over.
- **The way home.** Against the wind, with pirates off the capes of Cyprus. At Megiste, go by way of Patara, close under the cliffs, or beat straight across to Rhodes on rough water.

## Chapter IX: Alexandria

Autumn, 288 BC: the last voyage of the year, and of the game. Three days south across the open sea to Alexandria, the king of Egypt's new city, and home before the sea closes. About six to eight days at sea, and forty minutes to play.

- **The open sea in autumn.** Two nights under sail each way, cold enough to make men sick, and a gale may come up on the way out or on the way home. Holding on costs deck cargo, throwing costs heavy cargo, and running before it costs most of a day.
- **The coast of Egypt.** It is so low that ships find it by running onto it. Feel your way in with the lead, pay a fisherman to lead you in, or steer for the smoke of the tower being built on Pharos and hope it is not a village burning reeds. If you end up on a sandbank, throw cargo, pay to be towed off, or haul her off with the anchor. Petosiris, an Egyptian pilot for hire in Rhodes, knows the way.
- **Alexandria.** Forty-three years old and already the biggest city anyone aboard has seen. The king's customs men take a twentieth of the cargo. His storehouses sell grain, linen and papyrus; his dockyards buy fir spars, cedar and pitch, which Egypt cannot grow; his Museum buys books, any books; a wine merchant buys Rhodian wine; the palace buys luxuries; a trader from up the river sells ivory and a monkey; and the man building the tower on Pharos explains why it is needed.
- **The king's grain.** Sealed sacks for the grain commissioners of Rhodes, who pay well if it lands by the sixth day, and half if it is late.
- **The way home.** After the first night out, stand on for Rhodes, straight into the wind in the season of gales, or bear away for Cyprus and come home along Lycia, which is longer, makes the grain late, and passes Paphos, where the last copper of the year waits for the god's head.
- **The end.** The god gets his head. The last page lists every voyage, what each one earned, and where Ariston ended up.

Between chapters, your silver, any debt, the ship and everything in her, the crew, your good name in each port, and the friends you made all carry over. Hired hands go home at the end of each voyage.

## Project layout

- `src/game.html` is the whole game: HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file. It is a page fragment, with no `<html>` or `<body>` tags, so it can be published as a claude.ai artifact as it is.
- `index.html` is the same game wrapped as a standalone page. It is generated, so don't edit it by hand.
- `tools/build.mjs` creates `index.html` from `src/game.html`.
- `samples/` holds art samples, none of them part of the game:
  - `ports-detailed.html` and `ports-flat.html` draw Rhodes, Piraeus and Syracuse in two styles: detailed at 320 × 180 with shading ramps, coloured outlines and dithering, with a merchant close up; or flat at 224 × 126, the game's own look a step bigger, with each port's merchants as whole figures. `before-after.png` puts the detailed Rhodes beside the harbour as the game draws it.
  - `rhodes-iso.html` is the harbour as an isometric block, built from small cubes with black outlines and bright flat colours.
  - `game-iso.html` is the whole game with Rhodes, the voyage and the title page drawn that way, so the isometric style can be tried in play. As eBoy does it, the picture is full. In Rhodes: the old town, the quay and its stalls, a stoa, the lower town, two moles, a jetty, a dozen ships and boats, and crowds of people. At sea there is no land, only the open sea: your own ship in cubes (Elpis, Tyche or the grain ship) with the rose on her sail, on water where light wanders in a net of bright lines. The sun's glitter moves across the sea through the day, the ship and every boat cast a shadow away from it, long in the morning and evening, and clouds drift over with their shadows sliding across the water and the ship. Dolphins, gulls, other boats, and pirates or the king's patrol when they come. The sea is one flat bright colour with drawn ripples and wakes. There is no horizon and no sky: the world runs off every edge of the picture, and the time of day shows in the colour laid over all of it. Ports other than Rhodes, and the nights ashore, are still drawn the old way. It is built by `node tools/build-iso-preview.mjs` from `src/game.html` and `samples/iso-style.js`; rebuild it after changing either.
  - `game-2d.html` is the whole game with Rhodes in its usual side view, drawn with eBoy's tricks: every object outlined in black, flat bright colours, houses as little blocks with a front, a side and a roof, flat water with drawn ripples, and the harbour packed with ships, boats and people. The merchants stand bigger behind their counters. It is built by `node tools/build-iso-preview.mjs flat-style game-2d` from `src/game.html` and `samples/flat-style.js`.
  - `rhodes-painterly.html` is an earlier experiment with lit, painterly pixels at 400 × 225.
- `tests/sim.js` plays Chapters I to IX in turn, with bots that follow different strategies, over many seeds, to check the money and the risks. It has no dependencies.
- `tests/play.js` plays all nine chapters, to the last page, in a phone-sized Chromium through the real buttons, and saves screenshots to `tests/shots/`. It needs Playwright.

Inside `src/game.html`, the rules sit between `// ===== ENGINE START =====` and `// ===== ENGINE END =====` and never touch the page. That is what lets `tests/sim.js` run them on their own. All the art and sound is drawn and synthesized in code.

## Working on it

After editing `src/game.html`, regenerate the standalone page:

```bash
node tools/build.mjs
```

Check the balance (takes a few minutes; add a games count and a chapter, such as `node tests/sim.js src/game.html 100 4`, to check one chapter quickly):

```bash
node tests/sim.js
```

Play all nine chapters in a headless browser (takes about sixteen minutes):

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npx playwright install chromium
```

```bash
npm test
```
